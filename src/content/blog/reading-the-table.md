Pocketing the ball in front of you is only part of a turn. Before shooting, take a moment to notice where the other balls sit and what position the cue ball will need for the next shot. A simple plan can keep you from making a difficult recovery after a successful pot.

## Start with the easiest useful route

Look for a ball you can pocket with a clear path to a pocket, then identify a reasonable next ball. You do not need to plan every shot in the rack. Aiming one shot ahead is often enough to reveal whether the current pot leaves the cue ball in a useful part of the table or sends it toward a rail, traffic, or a blocked lane.

Check for obstacles along both the object-ball path and the cue-ball path. A ball can be easy to pocket but awkward to reach if another ball blocks the natural line. Also notice which pockets are available and whether another ball is already near the pocket you want.

## Choose a landing area, not just a point

You rarely need the cue ball to stop on one exact spot. Think instead of a patch of table from which the next shot has a comfortable angle. A wider landing area gives you room for small errors and often avoids needing a forceful or delicate stroke.

The cue ball's route depends on where it contacts the object ball and how much speed and spin you use. For a beginner, start with plain center-ball contact and modest speed. A gentle stun or natural roll can move the cue ball a manageable distance without adding side spin. If you use draw or follow, make sure you understand the contact point and have practiced the stroke; extra spin is not automatically a better plan.

## Respect rails and traffic

Estimate where the cue ball will travel after contact. It may keep rolling, stop near the collision, or rebound from a cushion depending on speed, contact, and spin. Treat these as practical estimates rather than guarantees: cloth, angle, and execution all affect the result.

When several balls crowd the same area, prefer a route that avoids sending the cue ball through traffic. A shorter, safer path is often easier to control than trying to cross the table for ideal position. If the route is blocked, reconsider the order of your balls or choose a shot that opens space.

## Review the result

After each shot, compare the actual cue-ball position with the area you intended to reach. If you finished too far away, note whether the speed, contact point, or chosen route caused it. Change one thing on the next attempt so you can learn what mattered.

Planning position is not about predicting every roll perfectly. It is about choosing a sensible next target, leaving yourself room for ordinary mistakes, and avoiding needless difficulty.
